How to Compare Written Business Financing Offers

When financing advice conflicts, a written offer is more useful than another general opinion. One person says to ask a bank; another says an online provider will move faster; a third recommends a specific program. None of those answers tells you what your business would receive, what it would owe, or when payments would be due. A fair comparison puts those numbers and conditions on one page.

The owner behind a small business financing question on Reddit described searching for weeks before applying for a truck and equipment purchase. The post is a useful example of uncertainty, not evidence that any product is approved or suitable. This guide starts one step later: once two or more providers have given you written terms, how do you read them without being distracted by speed or a low-looking payment?

Compare the same transaction

Begin with the purpose and amount. If one quote covers a $35,000 vehicle and equipment purchase while another covers only the vehicle, their payments cannot be compared directly. Write the asset price, taxes, fees, insurance and cash contribution in the same format for every provider. Record whether the lender pays the seller, sends funds to the business or requires reimbursement after purchase.

Note which terms are preliminary and which are final. An online estimate can be a useful starting point, but it may change after document review. Do not put an advertised example in the same column as a conditional approval and assume they carry equal weight. Mark each offer's stage: marketing example, estimate, prequalification, conditional offer or final agreement. Ask the provider what remains to be verified.

The Loan Services LLC guide to preparing a loan request covers the information to gather before applying. Once that file is organized, reuse it consistently. Sending different figures to different providers can create confusing comparisons and may slow review.

Start with funds actually received

The headline financing amount is not necessarily the cash the business can spend. Some agreements charge fees at closing or deduct them from proceeds. Others finance fees into the balance. A purchase requiring a down payment or seller deposit can create a cash need even when the approved amount seems to cover the invoice.

Write three figures in separate boxes: purchase cost, funds the provider will make available for that purchase, and cash the business must supply before the asset can be used. Ask whether insurance, registration, delivery or installation are included. For a truck purchase, the last mile of setup can be expensive enough to change the owner's decision. A business that closes with no reserve for payroll and fuel may have solved the invoice while weakening its operations.

If a provider says fees will be disclosed later, do not fill the comparison row with zero. Write “not yet provided” and wait for the actual terms. The FTC has addressed misleading claims about funding amounts and obligations in small-business financing. The practical borrower response is to ask for the amount received and every fee in writing.

Put total cost beside payment size

A lower periodic payment can result from a longer term. That may help the budget, but it can also increase total cost. The right column to compare is the sum of all required payments under the agreement, including disclosed fees, beside the net funds received. Ask the provider to identify any charges triggered by early payoff, late payment, renewal or modification.

Do not assume every product quotes interest in the same way. Term loans, lines of credit and sales-based financing can use different pricing language and repayment rules. If a quote uses a factor rate, fixed fee or other unfamiliar measure, ask for the total dollar obligation and a clear schedule. You may also ask an independent accountant or advisor to help translate unlike structures into a comparable cash-flow model. Avoid converting one rate to another with an online shortcut you do not understand.

Use the business's own budget to test the payment. A landscaping firm may collect more in warm months and less in winter. A payment that works only during peak season is a warning, even if the total cost looks competitive. The comparison must answer both questions: “What does this money cost?” and “Can the business carry the payment when cash is tight?”

Read timing and repayment frequency

“Fast funding” is meaningful only if the business has a real deadline. Record the date by which the seller needs payment and the date each provider can realistically fund a complete file. Ask which documents, inspection, insurance or title steps could delay closing. A promised quick decision is not the same as funds available to the seller.

Payment frequency deserves its own line. Monthly, weekly and daily withdrawals affect a business differently, even when the total amount paid is similar. Match withdrawal dates against deposits and payroll. If customers pay at the end of the month, a daily or weekly debit may require a larger operating cushion. Ask whether payment dates can be changed and whether automatic withdrawals are required.

Some products have a renewal or maturity event rather than a simple final installment. Record what is owed at that point and whether continued access depends on a new review. Do not treat a revolving credit limit as permanent cash in the bank. Also note whether the quoted rate can change during the term and what would cause that change.

Check guarantees and collateral

Collateral and personal guarantees can have consequences beyond the periodic payment. Ask exactly what assets secure the financing, whether a lien will be filed, whether the lender requires a guarantee from an owner, and what happens if the equipment is sold or replaced. The label “secured” or “unsecured” does not by itself explain the full set of obligations in a contract.

If the business is offered more money than it requested, ask why and recheck the downside. Extra proceeds can look like useful flexibility, but they also bring repayment duties. If a home or another personal asset is proposed as security, take time to understand that risk and consider independent legal advice. A lender should be able to explain its conditions before an owner signs.

Make a one-page comparison

Transaction coveredConfirms that both offers fund the same purchase.
Net funds availableShows cash actually usable after closing charges.

Total required paymentsReveals cost beyond a periodic payment.

Fees and early payoffShows costs that may appear later.

Conditions and funding dateSeparates a quote from a closable offer.

Leave a blank where a term is unknown rather than guessing. Send the missing questions back to the provider. This worksheet is not a scoring system that can choose for you. It makes the trade-offs visible. The offer with the lowest total cost may not meet a genuine deadline; the fastest may require payments the business cannot safely manage. The preferred choice must pass both the cost and cash-flow tests.

Review changes before signing

Final documents can differ from an early quote. Check the legal borrower name, amount, net proceeds, fee schedule, payment dates, collateral description and guarantee against your comparison page. If anything changes, revise the page and the cash-flow test. Do not sign because a deadline has made the paperwork feel routine.

Ask for a complete copy of the agreement and save the final schedule. Clarify how to make payments, whom to contact if an automatic debit fails, and how to obtain a payoff figure. A financing decision does not end at approval. The business needs to manage the obligation while the asset is in use. Frequently asked questions

Is the lowest monthly payment the cheapest offer?

Not necessarily. A longer term or additional fees can raise total cost. Compare all required payments and the amount actually received, then check whether the schedule fits the business.

Can I compare an estimate with a final agreement?

You can use the estimate to decide whether to continue, but mark it clearly as preliminary. Do not treat it as a firm alternative to signed or final written terms.

Does a quick approval mean the financing is affordable?

No. Approval reflects a provider's decision under its rules. The owner must still test payments against realistic revenue, payroll and other obligations.

What if two offers use different pricing terms?

Ask both providers for net proceeds, all fees, total required payments and a repayment schedule. If the structures remain hard to compare, obtain independent help before signing.
